# 14. Homes cost more every year; the tax relief on a home loan does not move

**More than half of new homes in India's big cities now sell above ₹1 crore. Buying one takes a bigger loan and more interest, but the tax relief on home-loan interest is a fixed ₹2 lakh, unchanged since 2014, and nothing at all under the new tax regime. The more an ordinary home costs, the smaller the share of its interest the tax system recognises.**

### Where homes are selling

In the first half of 2026, 54% of new homes sold across eight major cities cost more than ₹1 crore, up from 49% a year earlier. Homes under ₹50 lakh fell from 22% to 19% of sales \[1\].

| Price of the home | Share of sales, H1 2025 | Share of sales, H1 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under ₹50 lakh | 22% | 19% |
| ₹50 lakh–₹1 crore | 29% | 27% |
| ₹1–2 crore | 27% | 29% |
| Above ₹2 crore | 22% | 25% |

The report itself explains part of the shift: homes that used to sell for ₹50 lakh–₹1 crore have been repriced into the ₹1–2 crore band after three to four years of rising prices \[1\]. The data covers new homes sold in eight cities, not all housing in India, and it has no break at ₹1.5 crore.

### Prices keep rising

The Reserve Bank's all-India house price index, built from registered sales in 18 cities, rose 4.2% in the year to January–March 2026 \[2\]. In the large cities the rise was often faster: over the 12 months to H1 2026, average prices of new homes rose 18% in Delhi and Faridabad, 15% in Ghaziabad, 9% in Bengaluru, 8% in Noida, 7% in Hyderabad and 5% in Mumbai, Pune, Chennai and Kolkata \[1\].

### How much of your interest the tax system recognises

On a home you live in, the old regime lets you deduct at most ₹2 lakh of interest a year, the limit set in July 2014 \[3\]\[4\]. The new regime allows none \[4\]. Here is what that means at today's prices, for a home bought with an 80% loan over 20 years at 8.5% (our calculation):

| Home price | Loan | Interest in the first year | Share of that interest the tax system recognises: old regime | New regime |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ₹50 lakh | ₹40 lakh | ₹3.37 lakh | 59% | 0% |
| ₹1 crore | ₹80 lakh | ₹6.74 lakh | 30% | 0% |
| ₹1.5 crore | ₹1.2 crore | ₹10.11 lakh | 20% | 0% |
| ₹2 crore | ₹1.6 crore | ₹13.48 lakh | 15% | 0% |

As prices rise, the fixed limit covers less: someone who needs a ₹1.5 crore home to live where they work gets relief on a fifth of their interest under the old regime, and none under the new one. A home bought to let out fares no better: since 2017 at most ₹2 lakh of its loss can be set against other income in the old regime, and none in the new (Article 12).

### Why it discourages buying, not only owning

The two problems compound for a new buyer. The price of an ordinary home in a large city keeps climbing, so the loan does too; meanwhile the rupee limits on relief stay where they were set years ago (Article 13, fiscal drag). Each year, the tax system shares less of the cost of buying a home with the people buying them.

> How to read this: the price bands are new homes sold in eight cities, from a property consultancy's research; the share of interest is our illustration with stated assumptions, not tax advice. The ₹2 lakh limit applies to the old regime; principal repayments may separately count within the ₹1.5 lakh section 80C limit.
