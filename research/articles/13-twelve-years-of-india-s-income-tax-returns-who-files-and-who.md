# 13. Twelve years of India's income tax returns: who files, and who pays

**Between AY 2012-13 and AY 2023-24 the number of individual tax returns grew 2.6 times, yet most filers still pay no income tax, and the tax that is paid has become more concentrated: by AY 2023-24, half of all individual income tax came from 1.4% of returns.**

Every figure below is calculated from the Central Board of Direct Taxes' own *Income Tax Return Statistics*, one report for each assessment year from 2012-13 to 2023-24 \[1\]\[2\]. An assessment year covers income earned in the year before: AY 2023-24 is income of April 2022 to March 2023.

### More people file, and more of them earn a salary

Individual returns rose from 2,89,25,598 in AY 2012-13 to 7,54,61,286 in AY 2023-24, 2.6 times as many. Returns reporting a salary rose faster, from 1,17,49,688 to 3,79,64,804, 3.2 times as many \[1\]\[2\].

### Most people who file pay no income tax

The share of individual returns with no tax payable fell from 56.4% in AY 2012-13 to 40.5% in AY 2018-19. Then it jumped to 68.7% in AY 2020-21 and has stayed above 60% since; in AY 2023-24 it was 62.7% \[1\]\[2\].

The jump coincides with the 2019 interim budget, which raised the rebate so that taxable income up to ₹5 lakh paid no tax from FY 2019-20, the income year of AY 2020-21, without changing the slabs \[3\].

### Tax is paid by a shrinking share of filers

Returns with tax payable of ₹10 lakh or more were 0.53% of individual returns in AY 2012-13 and paid 40.9% of all individual income tax. By AY 2023-24 they were 1.4% of returns and paid 50.3% \[1\]\[2\].

### Inflation and fixed lines

Prices rose 87% over these years (consumer price index \[4\]), but the rupee lines in the tax system, such as a ₹10 lakh tax bill or a ₹50 lakh salary, stayed where they were. So more people cross them without being better off in real terms: salaried filers above ₹50 lakh rose 8.4 times, from 53,659 to 4,52,128 \[1\]\[2\], but ₹50 lakh in 2011-12 bought about what ₹93 lakh bought in 2022-23 \[4\].

The salary at which the top 1% of salaried filers begins rose from about ₹37 lakh to ₹61 lakh in rupees of the time. Adjusted for inflation it fell, from about ₹69 lakh (in 2022-23 rupees) to ₹61 lakh: the top of the salaried ladder has not outrun prices \[1\]\[2\]\[4\].

### Fiscal drag: the limits that did not move

When a tax limit is fixed in rupees and prices rise, the limit buys less every year, so people pay more tax without any law changing. Economists call this *fiscal drag* or *bracket creep*.

Four limits for the old tax regime were set in the Budget of 10 July 2014 \[5\] and are still the same today \[6\]. Prices rose 62% between 2014-15 and 2024-25 \[4\]:

| Limit (old regime) | Set in July 2014, still today | If it had followed prices | What it is worth today, in 2014 rupees |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Income exempt from tax (under 60) | ₹2.5 lakh | ₹4.04 lakh | ₹1.55 lakh |
| Income exempt from tax (senior citizens) | ₹3 lakh | ₹4.85 lakh | ₹1.86 lakh |
| Section 80C investments (PPF, EPF, ELSS, life cover) | ₹1.5 lakh | ₹2.42 lakh | ₹0.93 lakh |
| Home-loan interest, self-occupied house | ₹2 lakh | ₹3.23 lakh | ₹1.24 lakh |

The new regime's tax-free slab, ₹4 lakh since 2025-26 \[6\], is close to where the 2014 exemption would be if it had followed prices. People who stay in the old regime to claim 80C or home-loan interest still work with limits worth about 38% less than when they were set.

India's income tax law does not adjust any of these limits for inflation; they change only when a Budget changes them. Whether they should follow prices automatically is a policy choice.
